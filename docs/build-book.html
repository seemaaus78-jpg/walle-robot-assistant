A build book for a first-time maker

Build a robot that listens and answers

You do not need to know any coding. You do not need to know what any of the parts are. This book explains every single thing, in order, from the very beginning.

First

What you are making

A small box that you can talk to.

You say a city name out loud, and it tells you about that city — where it is, how many people live there. You ask it to translate, and it speaks your words in another language.

The special part: it works with no internet. All the knowledge sits on a memory card inside it. On a plane, on a mountain, in another country with no signal — it still works.

If there is internet, it also becomes cleverer and can answer almost anything, like a phone assistant.

How long will this take?

Give yourself three weekends. One to buy things and learn to solder. One to connect the parts. One to put the software on. Nobody does this in an afternoon, and that is normal.

Chapter 1

The parts you need to buy

There are twelve things — six that make it think and talk, and six that make it move. Below is a drawing of each one, what it does, and why we picked it.

The drawings show the little metal pins on each part, because later you will need to find them by name.

Radxa Cubie A7Z

1 GB or 2 GB version · roughly $30–45

This is the brain. It is a whole little computer — it runs Linux, the same kind of software that runs most of the internet. Everything else plugs into it.

The row of 40 little metal pins along the top edge is how you attach the other parts. Each pin has a job.

Official page → Search shops

Why this one: it is small, and it has enough memory to hold the speech and translation software. A cheaper chip like an Arduino or ESP32 is far too small — those need the internet for everything, and the whole point here is working without internet.

INMP441 microphone

roughly $4–8 · SPH0645 also works

This is the ear. It hears you speak.

It is a digital microphone. That means it turns your voice into numbers right there on the little board, and sends the numbers to the brain.

Search shops → AliExpress

Why digital: a cheap normal microphone sends a very weak signal down the wire, and it picks up buzzing and hiss from the computer next to it. This one sends numbers instead, and numbers cannot get fuzzy. Your voice arrives clean.

MAX98357A amplifier

$5.95 at Adafruit · cheaper copies exist

This is the voice box. The brain sends it numbers, and it turns them back into sound and makes them loud enough to hear.

A speaker on its own is far too quiet. This part gives it the push it needs.

Adafruit — $5.95 → Search shops

Why this one: it does two jobs in one small board — it turns numbers into sound and makes it loud. Most setups need two separate parts for that.

Small speaker

roughly $3–6 · "8 ohm 2 watt"

This is the mouth. It makes the actual sound you hear.

"8 ohm" is just the type. Ask for an 8 ohm speaker and you will get the right thing.

Search shops →

Why small: the whole robot is meant to sit on a desk. A big speaker would need a bigger battery and a bigger box, for volume you do not need.

MicroSD memory card

32 GB or 64 GB, Class 10 and A1 · roughly $8–14

This is the memory. It holds everything: the operating system, the voice software, and the list of 200,000 cities.

Look for A1 printed on the card. This is not a detail you can skip.

Search shops →

Why A1 matters: A1 cards are fast at finding lots of small pieces of information, which is exactly what your robot does all day. A cheap card without A1 makes it pause and stutter in the middle of sentences. It is the most common reason a build feels broken when it is not.

Jumper wires

a pack of 40 · roughly $5

These connect the parts together. You push them on — no soldering needed.

Get the kind with a little plastic socket (a hole) on both ends. These are sold as "female to female".

Search shops →

Why start with these: you can pull them off and try again. Get everything working with jumper wires first. Only solder later, when you already know it all works.

USB webcam

any cheap one · roughly $10–20

This is the eye. It lets the robot look at something and tell you what it is.

Point it at a menu in a language you cannot read and ask "what does this say in English". Point it at a street and ask "what do you see".

Search shops →

Why USB and not the ribbon kind: a ribbon camera has a delicate flat cable and needs extra settings turned on. A USB webcam just plugs in. For a first project, take the easy one — you can always change later.

Parts that make it move

These give the robot its body — the tracks it drives on and the frame that holds everything.

Gear motors × 2

"N20 gear motor 6V 30RPM" · roughly $4–8 each

These are the legs. One for each track. They turn slowly and with a lot of force, which is what you want — a fast motor makes the robot skitter and fall over.

Buy two the same. Two different speeds means it curves instead of going straight.

AliExpress → Amazon

What "gear motor" means: a plain motor spins very fast and very weakly. A gear motor has a little gearbox on the front that trades that speed for strength. Without one the robot would whirr and not move.

Mini L298N motor driver

roughly $2–5 · also sold as "L298N mini dual H-bridge"

This is the muscle. It sits between the brain and the motors.

The brain can only whisper — a few milliamps. The motors need a shove — hundreds. This board takes the whisper as an instruction and delivers the shove from the battery.

AliExpress → Amazon

Never skip this. Wiring a motor straight to a brain pin destroys the pin, and usually the whole board. It is the single most common way a first project dies. The driver exists precisely to stop that.

Toy chain and sprockets

"model tank track" or "toy roller chain" · roughly $5–10

These are the tracks — the caterpillar treads that make it look like WALL·E rather than a box on wheels.

You need the chain and two toothed wheels (sprockets) per side for it to run around.

AliExpress →

Getting the tension right: the chain should turn freely with a little slack. Too tight and the motors stall and flatten your battery in minutes. Too loose and it falls off every time the robot reverses.

Copper wire and brass tube

1 mm copper wire · 1.5 mm brass tube · roughly $8 together

This is the skeleton. You bend the copper wire by hand into the shape of the robot and solder the corners.

The brass tube is stiffer. It makes the axles the track wheels spin on, and the copper wire slides inside it.

Copper wire → Brass tube

Why copper and not plastic: copper is soft enough to bend by hand and hard enough to hold its shape once bent. It also solders beautifully, so the joints become part of the structure. And it looks like the video.

Battery and charger

3.7 V 1000–2000 mAh LiPo + TP4056 with protection · roughly $10

This is what makes it cordless. Add it last, once everything already works on a wall charger.

Charger → Battery

The word "protection" is not optional. The protected version has two extra chips on it that stop the battery being drained too far or short-circuited. The bare version has neither, and a damaged lithium battery can catch fire. Read the listing title carefully.

You also need a USB-C power supply (a phone charger that gives 5 volts and 3 amps — check the small print on it) and a computer with an SD card slot or a USB card reader.

Chapter 2

Two things that can hurt you

Everything else in this project is safe. These two are not, so please read this part.

The soldering iron

If you solder later, the metal tip is about 350 degrees. That is hotter than an oven, and it does not look hot. Rules: put it back in its stand every single time. Open a window. If it falls, let it fall — never try to catch it.

Batteries (only if you add one later)

Start with a wall charger. If you add a rechargeable battery later, buy one that says "with protection". These batteries hold a lot of energy and can catch fire if damaged. Never bend one, never poke it, and if it ever swells up or feels hot, put it outside on concrete and do not charge it again.

If you ever join two batteries together

People sometimes connect two batteries side by side (in parallel) to make the robot last longer. It works, but there is a rule nobody tells you:

Both batteries must be identical, and both must be at the same charge level when you join them.

If one is full and the other is half empty, the moment you connect them a very large current pours from the full one into the empty one. That can overheat them both. Charge them separately to the same level first, or simply use one bigger battery instead — which is easier and just as good.

Good news

The first version of this project uses only push-on jumper wires and a wall charger. No soldering, no battery. You can build the whole working robot before you touch either of those things.

Chapter 3

How electricity works, very simply

Think of water in pipes.

Voltage is the pressure in the pipe. We measure it in volts. Our parts want either 3.3 volts or 5 volts.

Ground is the pipe that brings the water back. Every part needs one. On the boards it is written GND.

The most important rule in this whole book

Every part's GND must connect to the brain's GND.

If you forget this, nothing works — and there is no error, no smoke, no warning. It just sits there silently. This is the number one thing that confuses beginners, and now you already know it.

3.3 and 5 are not the same

The microphone needs 3.3 volts. Giving it 5 volts can break it permanently. When you connect the wires, check this one twice.

Chapter 4

Connecting the parts

You will push jumper wires between the brain's 40 pins and the two little boards. Twelve wires in total.

Before you touch anything

Unplug the power. Always connect wires with the power off. Then plug in.

First: find the pins

The 40 pins have numbers. Pin 1 is the one in the corner — most boards print a tiny "1" or a small square next to it. From there the numbers zig-zag: pin 1 and 2 side by side, then 3 and 4, and so on.

Some pins always mean the same thing on every board like this:

Pin numberWhat it gives
1 and 173.3 volts
2 and 45 volts
6, 9, 14, 20, 25, 30, 34, 39GND (ground) — any of them will do

Then: the four sound pins

Four more pins carry the sound. These four are different on different boards, so you must look them up for yours — see the warning below.

PartIts pinGoes to

Why two parts share the same wire

Look again: the microphone's SCK and the amplifier's BCLK go to the same pin. So do WS and LRC.

That is on purpose. Those two wires are a shared heartbeat that keeps the ear and the mouth in time with each other. Only the wires carrying the actual sound are separate — one coming in, one going out.

You must look up four pins yourself

I do not know exactly which pins carry sound on your board, and I will not guess, because a wrong guess can damage it.

Find them in two places: search the internet for "Radxa Cubie A7Z pinout" and look at the official picture, and type gpioinfo on the board once it is running. Look for pins named with I2S in them. Five minutes of checking saves you a broken board.

Two pins that give silence instead of an error

Microphone L/R: if you leave this pin unconnected, the microphone sends nothing at all and reports no problem. Connect it to a GND pin.

Amplifier SD: despite the name, this is not an on/off switch. If it is left unconnected the amplifier stays silent. Check the instructions that came with your amplifier board for which pin to connect it to.

Test — do this before going on

Nothing soldered, just wires pushed on. Plug in the power. Nothing should get hot, and no burning smell. If something feels warm, unplug it right away and check every wire again.

Chapter 4b

Building the body

Now the part that makes it a robot and not a box.

About jumper wires and copper wire

You will use both, at different times, and this is on purpose.

Jumper wires first. They push on and pull off, so you can get everything working and fix mistakes without a soldering iron. Do all your testing like this.

Copper wire last. In the video, the copper wire is not just wiring — it is the robot's frame. The same piece of copper holds the shape and carries the electricity. That is why the finished one looks so neat and has no loose cables. But you only want to solder it once you already know which wire goes where.

Step by step

1

Print the shape

The person who designed this robot gives away a paper template. Print it at 100 percent — not "fit to page", which quietly shrinks it and then nothing lines up.

2

Bend the copper wire on top of the paper

Lay the wire on the printed line and bend it with pliers to follow. Copper is soft and stays where you put it.

Try to get each bend right the first time. If you bend and straighten the same spot over and over it becomes hard and snaps.

3

Solder the corners

Copper pulls heat away fast, so hold the iron on longer than feels right and use plenty of flux. If a joint looks starved and grey rather than smooth and shiny, it is starved — reheat it.

4

Make the axles from brass tube

Roll a craft knife around the tube to cut it, rather than squeezing it with cutters, which crushes the end shut.

5

Fit the sprockets and chain

The chain should turn freely with a little slack. Too tight stalls the motors. Too loose and it jumps off when reversing.

6

Mount the motors so the sprockets line up

If a sprocket is even slightly out of line with the chain, the track will come off every time the robot changes direction.

Your frame is made of metal, and metal conducts

The copper frame carries electricity. Anything you bolt to it must not touch it directly, or you will short something out. Put tape between every board and the frame where they meet.

Connecting the motors

The motors do not connect to the brain. They connect to the driver board, and the driver board connects to the brain.

FromToWhy
Left motor, 2 wiresDriver "MOTOR A"Power for the left track
Right motor, 2 wiresDriver "MOTOR B"Power for the right track
Driver IN1 IN2 IN3 IN44 brain pinsThe instructions
Driver VCC and GNDBattery + and −Where the real power comes from
Driver GNDBrain GND tooRule 1 — they must share ground
Never connect a motor straight to the brain

A brain pin can whisper. A motor needs a shove. Connect them directly and the motor pulls far more electricity than the pin can give, and the board dies. This is the most common way a first project ends.

That is the entire reason the driver board exists. Always go through it.

Test — drive the bare frame

Before you add any of the sound or camera parts, test just this: motors, driver, battery, brain. It should go forward, backward, and spin on the spot.

Finding a fault now, with four wires connected, is easy. Finding it later inside a finished robot is miserable.

Chapter 5

Putting the software on the card

Now you fill the memory card. You will type some lines into a black window on your computer. You do not need to understand them. Copy each one, paste it, press Enter, wait for it to finish.

I will tell you what each one does in one sentence.

1

Put Linux on the card

Download a free program called Balena Etcher. Download the Radxa operating system file. Etcher copies it onto the card. Pick the version with no desktop — no icons, no mouse. That version leaves much more room for your robot's software.

While Etcher is open, type in your Wi-Fi name and password if it asks. It saves you trouble later.

2

Turn it on and connect from your computer

Put the card in the board, plug in the power. Wait two minutes for the first start.

The robot has no screen. You talk to it from your own computer using a black window (Terminal on Mac, PowerShell on Windows):

ssh radxa@radxa.local

Everything from here on gets typed into that window.

3

Turn on the sound connection

The board does not listen to the microphone until you switch that ability on. Type:

sudo rsetup

A blue menu appears. Go to Overlays, find the entry with I2S in the name, turn it on. Then restart the board.

4

Check the ear and mouth are found

arecord -l aplay -l

The first line should mention your microphone. The second should mention your amplifier. If either says "no soundcards found", the step above did not work — go back and try a different overlay name.

Test — record your own voice
arecord -D default -f S16_LE -r 16000 -c 1 -d 5 /tmp/test.wav aplay /tmp/test.wav

The first line records five seconds. Say something. The second plays it back through the speaker.

If you hear yourself, the hard part is done. The rest is just installing software. If you hear nothing, stop here and fix it — nothing later will work until this does.

5

Install the helper programs

sudo apt update sudo apt install -y python3-venv python3-dev portaudio19-dev alsa-utils git

This downloads the basic tools the robot's software needs. It takes a few minutes.

6

Download the robot's software

git clone https://github.com/seemaaus78-jpg/walle-robot-assistant.git ~/travel_assistant cd ~/travel_assistant python3 -m venv .venv source .venv/bin/activate pip install --no-cache-dir -r requirements.txt

Line by line: fetch the software, go into its folder, make a clean space for it, step into that space, install the pieces it needs.

7

Download the voices and the ears

./scripts/fetch_models.sh

This downloads about 250 MB: the software that understands speech, and the voices that speak back. Have a cup of tea.

8

Build the city list

Do this bit on your own computer, not on the robot — it is much faster there, then you copy the finished file across. The instructions are in the file docs/setup.md that came with the software.

It makes one file, about 50 MB, holding 200,000 cities with their country, size and position.

Test — check the brain without the microphone
python3 main_assistant.py --text "tell me about tokyo"

This skips the microphone completely and just asks the question directly. It should print the answer about Tokyo.

This is a clever trick worth remembering: it separates "is the thinking working?" from "is the listening working?" — so when something breaks, you know which half to look at.

Chapter 6

Making it clever online (optional)

Your robot already works. This step makes it able to answer almost anything when it has Wi-Fi.

Google gives out a free key that lets your robot ask its AI questions. Get one from Google AI Studio, then type:

export GEMINI_API_KEY=paste-your-key-here
Keep it private

That key is like a password. Do not put it in a photo, a message, or anywhere public. If someone else gets it they can use it and it counts against you.

With no key, or with no Wi-Fi, the robot simply carries on using what is on the card. It never stops working — you just get shorter answers.

Chapter 6a

Teaching it to see

Plug in the webcam. That is the whole setup.

You will need one small program installed so the robot can take a photo. Type this once:

sudo apt install -y fswebcam

Now the robot can look at things when you ask.

Ask it to look

"what do you see" Let me look. … A narrow street of wooden houses, with lanterns strung between the balconies.

Ask it to read something

This is the useful one when travelling. Hold up a menu, a sign, a train ticket:

"read this" Ramen 850 yen. Gyoza 400 yen. Green tea free.

Ask it to read AND translate

The best trick it does. Point it at writing in a language you cannot read:

"what does this menu say in english" Ramen, 850 yen. Dumplings, 400 yen. Green tea is free.
It only takes a photo when you ask

The camera is not always watching. It does not record. It takes one single photo at the moment you ask it to look, and nothing else.

It also says "Let me look" out loud first, so you always know when a photo is being taken. And the photo is not kept — it is sent, used to answer you, and thrown away.

That is on purpose. A robot that listens is one thing. A camera quietly watching a room is a different thing, and this is not that.

Seeing needs internet — this part cannot work offline

Understanding a photo takes far more memory than your little computer has. So this is the one feature that needs Wi-Fi.

With no internet it simply tells you: "I need an internet connection to see." It will not guess at what is in front of it and pretend. Everything else — cities, translation, saved guides — keeps working with no internet as normal.

Chapter 6b

It remembers what it learns

This is the clever part.

When your robot has internet and you ask about a city, it does not just answer. It asks Google's AI for a full travel guide — good areas to walk, places to eat, train stations, where to stay, the emergency number and main hospitals — and then writes all of it onto the memory card.

Later, with no internet at all, that guide is still there. Ask again on the plane and it tells you the same things.

Why this is worth doing

Look up the cities you are going to before you travel, while you still have Wi-Fi at home. Then the robot carries all of it in your bag, and works in a country where your phone does not.

Ask for a guide

"travel guide for kyoto" Kyoto is the old capital of Japan, full of temples and quiet lanes. I can also tell you about areas to visit, food and restaurants, getting around, where to stay and emergency and health. I have saved it, so you will have it without internet.

Then ask about one part of it

"what are the restaurants in kyoto" In Kyoto, for food and restaurants: Nishiki Market for street food. Pontocho Alley for evening dinner. This guide was saved today.

You can ask about restaurants, areas to visit, trains and stations, hotels, or hospitals and police.

It always tells you how old the guide is

Notice it said "saved today". A list of restaurants is not a permanent fact — places close and change. So the robot always tells you when it collected the information, and if it is more than three months old it warns you that it may be wrong now.

Do not trust the emergency information

When you ask about hospitals or police, the robot adds a warning to its answer, and it means it.

The AI can invent a hospital address that sounds completely real. For a restaurant that is annoying. In an emergency it is dangerous. Always check emergency information with a real local source — a hotel desk, a sign, a paper map.

Deleting what you no longer need

Just tell it:

"forget kyoto" Deleted my guide for Kyoto. "delete all guides" Deleted all 12 saved guides.

To see what it is holding, say "what have you saved".

If you would rather do it with the keyboard, there is a small tool for it:

python3 scripts/guides.py list python3 scripts/guides.py show kyoto python3 scripts/guides.py delete kyoto python3 scripts/guides.py clear
Nothing you save can break the robot

The guides are kept in their own separate file, away from the main list of cities. So deleting all of them — even deleting the file by hand — cannot damage anything. The robot simply starts a fresh empty one next time it turns on.

Chapter 7

Talking to your robot

Just speak normally.

Ask about a city

"tell me about kyoto" Kyoto is in Kyoto, Japan. It has a population of roughly 1.4 million people.

You can also say "how big is Paris" or "where is Bogota". You do not have to pronounce accents perfectly.

Translate

Say which languages you want, once, in your own language:

"translate from english to spanish" Translating english to spanish. Go ahead.

Now just talk, and everything you say comes back in Spanish:

"where is the train station" ¿dónde está la estación de tren?

It keeps doing that until you ask for something different. To change, just say "speak french" or "translate from english to german".

Make it move

"go forward" · "go back" · "turn left" · "turn right" · "stop"

It moves for about a second and then stops on its own. That is deliberate — there is nothing on this robot that can see the edge of a table, so it never keeps going indefinitely.

"Stop" jumps the queue

Every other thing you say might take a moment while it thinks or asks the internet. "Stop" never does. It goes straight to the motors ahead of everything else.

If your robot is rolling toward the edge of a desk, a two second pause while it contacts Google is exactly the wrong behaviour. So stopping was built to skip all of that.

It does not say anything when it moves. Talking over its own motors would just be noise.

Other things to say

Say thisIt does
"lets talk"Ordinary conversation
"switch to city guide"Back to answering about cities
"system status"Tells you if it has internet and which languages it is using
"what can you do"Lists everything it knows how to do
"shut down"Turns itself off safely
It tells the truth when it cannot help

If you ask for a language it has not downloaded, it says so plainly in English. It will never pretend — it will not read English words in a Spanish accent and hope you do not notice. For a translator, quietly getting it wrong is the worst thing it could do.

Chapter 8

When something does not work

Everyone hits these. None of them mean you did anything wrong.

What you seeWhat to check first
Nothing happens at allThe GND wires. Every part must share ground with the brain. This is the answer more often than anything else.
The microphone records silenceThe L/R pin is not connected. Connect it to a GND pin.
No sound from the speakerThe SD pin on the amplifier. It is a settings pin, not an on/off switch.
The board does not find the sound partsThe I2S setting is not switched on. Go back to Chapter 5, step 3.
The voice sounds too fast or too slowA small settings file is missing next to the voice file. Run the download script again.
It answers its own voiceMove the microphone further from the speaker, or turn the volume down.
It is slow just after switching onNormal. It is loading the translation part into memory. It only happens once.
Only one track movesCheck that motor's two wires at the driver board. One is probably loose.
It turns instead of going straightOne motor is wired the other way round. Swap that motor's two wires.
The motors buzz but do not turnEither the chain is too tight, or the battery is low. Buzzing is a stalled motor.
The track falls off when reversingThe sprockets are not lined up, or the chain is too loose.
It restarts whenever it movesThe motors are pulling the power down. Give them their own battery connection, not the brain's.
The camera gives a black pictureSome webcams need a moment to wake up. Set warmup_s = 0.5 in the settings file.
It says it cannot seeEither no webcam is plugged in, or there is no internet. Seeing needs both.
It cannot find any cityThe city file was not copied onto the robot. Check world_cities.db is there and about 50 MB.
How to ask for help well

When you get stuck, write down three things: what you expected, what actually happened, and the exact words of any message you saw. That turns a hard question into an easy one for whoever helps you.

An honest note. The software in this project is real and tested — 350 automatic checks run on it. But nobody has yet built this exact robot with these exact parts.
