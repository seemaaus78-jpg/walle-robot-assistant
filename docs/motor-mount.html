WALL·E build — the drive

Motor into track

Your track is already a working assembly: two sprockets screwed to a steel arm, spinning on those screws as axles. Driving it means replacing one screw with a motor shaft — and making the sprocket grip that shaft, which is the whole difficulty.

Exploded along the shaft

Making the sprocket grip

This is the part that fails. The sprocket's bore is sized for a screw, the shaft is a 3 mm D. Four ways to join them:

Never glue with the shaft's flat hidden.

A round shaft in a round hole is held only by friction, and epoxy in shear on a smooth cylinder peels. Rotate the shaft so the flat sits where the glue is thickest — that turns a friction joint into a mechanical key.

Order of work

  1. Take the screw out of the big sprocket only. Leave the idler's screw alone — it is that sprocket's axle and it has to keep spinning.
  2. Check the bore against the shaft. N20 shafts are 3 mm. If the sprocket's hole is smaller, open it with a 3 mm bit, slowly, with the sprocket clamped.
  3. Drill the frame rail for clearance. Slightly larger than 3 mm. The shaft spins inside this hole — it is a bearing, not a grip. Anything tight here and the motor stalls.
  4. Lash the gearbox to the rail with copper. Through both mounting holes, around the rail, soldered. The motor must not be able to twist — all its torque tries to rotate the body instead of the track.
  5. Couple the shaft, then leave it overnight. Grub screw onto the flat, or epoxy keyed against it. Do not test it early; half-cured epoxy fails and you start again.
  6. Spin it by hand before any power. The chain should move with light finger pressure. Stiff means the idler is binding or the clearance hole is too tight. Fix it now, not after both sides are built.
Both drive sprockets at the same height.

Measure from the bottom rail to the shaft centre on each side before committing. Two millimetres of difference makes the robot lean and one track slip.
